# Seasonal Celebration-Path Research Notes

## Design Guardrails

The child experience should use simple, calm visual language and avoid naming holidays in its on-screen prompts. The **parent settings** can identify the inspiration and explain the choice. Each path should be selectable independently rather than blending traditions into one generic winter event.

| Parent-facing selection | Child-facing trail title | Appropriate motif direction | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Halloween-inspired | Pumpkin & lantern trail | Friendly pumpkins, warm lanterns, felt bats, moonlit path, cozy costumes for plushies only | Scares, danger, horror, threats, jump moments, or urgency. |
| Thanksgiving-inspired | Harvest & kindness trail | Leaves, squash, corn, baskets, sharing and gratitude care actions | Pilgrim-and-“Indian” imagery, costumes, feathers, or claims about a simplified “first Thanksgiving.” |
| Christmas-inspired | Winter sparkle trail | Evergreen boughs, warm lights, stockings, wrapped kindness notes, cozy red-and-green felt details | Treating the setting as a universal winter default or combining it with another religious tradition. |
| Hanukkah-inspired | Lights & kindness trail | A distinct blue-and-gold path, a nine-branched hanukkiyah visual, and a dreidel; gentle increasing-light motif | Calling the hanukkiyah a generic seven-branched menorah, merging it with Christmas imagery, or treating religious symbols as random prizes. |

## Source-Based Notes

The Union for Reform Judaism describes Hanukkah as an eight-day Jewish Festival of Lights, with candles and light central to its observance. Its resource distinguishes the **hanukkiyah** and references the dreidel as a customary family activity. The Hanukkah path should therefore use those motifs accurately and remain a distinct parent-selected cultural option.[1]

The Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian’s Thanksgiving resources recommend approaching harvest and giving-thanks themes through environment, community, and year-round perspectives, while specifically offering culturally sensitive resources that move beyond simplified origin stories. The harvest path should consequently focus on kindness, sharing, garden abundance, and seasonal care—not Indigenous stereotypes or costume imagery.[2]
